The Last Lighthouse Keeper of Skerry Point

By Tom Hale

For forty years, Margaret Reid climbed the one hundred and twelve steps of the tower every evening, wound the clockwork, trimmed the wick, and watched the beam sweep across the water. She kept a log of every ship, every storm, and every night the fog came in.

“The light never went out,” she says, “not once, not in forty years.” Her logbooks, now in the county museum, fill two shelves and record more than eleven thousand passing vessels.

A life measured in watches

The keeper's day was divided into watches of four hours. In winter, when the nights were long, she and her husband shared them; in summer, the work was lighter, but the visitors came, asking to see the lamp, the lens, and the view from the gallery.

The sea does not care who is watching. You keep the light because someone might need it.

Her duties, as written in the service rules of 1921, were simple:

  • Light the lamp at sunset and put it out at sunrise.
  • Keep the lens clean, and the brass polished.
    • Use only soft cloths on the lens.
  • Record the weather every four hours.

The automation

In 1998 the light was automated. The steps for the changeover were these:

  1. Install the electric lamp.
  2. Fit the remote monitoring.
  3. Remove the clockwork.

The control code of the new lamp is short:

if (dusk) {
    lamp.on();
}

Today the beam still sweeps the water at Skerry Point, ten seconds for each turn, and H2O still beats against the rocks below. Margaret Reid lives in the village now, where she can see the light from her kitchen window, and she says it is the best view in the world.
